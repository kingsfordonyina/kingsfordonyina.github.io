Welfare Reform and the Noncognitive Skill Formation of the Next Generation

Job market paper, 2026 · JEL: I38, J13, J24, H53

View latest version
Noncognitive skills (NCS) are an important dimension of human capital and are shaped by early-life environments. While early-childhood interventions can have lasting effects on these skills, little is known about the long-run consequences of social safety-net reform for NCS. I examine the effects of early-life exposure to the 1990s U.S. welfare reform. Using the Panel Study of Income Dynamics core survey and its Child Development and Transition into Adulthood Supplements, I exploit variation in reform exposure across states, birth cohorts, and mothers' likelihood of being affected in a triple-difference design. I find that early-life exposure reduced young-adult NCS by 0.24 standard deviations among individuals born to mothers most likely to be affected. Suggestive mechanism evidence is more consistent with changes in the organization of early care than with changes in household economic resources, with reform increasing reliance on formal childcare during a sensitive developmental period. The harm is substantially smaller where state work-exemption rules protected mothers of infants. Safety-net reforms can therefore leave lasting developmental effects that conventional economic outcomes may not capture.

Does Expanding SNAP Eligibility Reduce Crime? Evidence from Broad-Based Categorical Eligibility

Onyina, K. · Working paper, 2026 · Draft available upon request

Eligibility rules determine who can enter a public assistance program, yet their consequences outside it remain poorly understood. Existing evidence linking SNAP to crime studies the timing, delivery, or withdrawal of benefits among households that already qualify. I examine Broad-Based Categorical Eligibility (BBCE), which permits states to relax SNAP asset and income screens while the federal benefit formula remains unchanged. Combining USDA policy and participation data with FBI UCR arrest records, I exploit variation in the timing and scope of state adoption in a stacked difference-in-differences design. BBCE increases SNAP participation by 8 percent and reduces adult arrests by about 12 percent across the total, violent, and property offense indices, with declines concentrated in aggravated assault, rape, and larceny. Results hold across alternative comparison groups, estimators, samples, and an in-time placebo, although violent-arrest estimates are sensitive to differential linear trends. Counterfactual estimates imply 15 million additional SNAP person-years and 239,000 fewer index arrests across sampled agencies. Eligibility design shapes not only who participates but also who comes into contact with the criminal justice system.

Cash Transfer and the Labor Supply of Vulnerable Households and Individuals: Does Household Indebtedness Matter?

Onyina, K., & Turkson, D. (2024). Working Paper.

View latest version
Cash transfer programs are central to poverty reduction, yet their effects on labor supply remain contested in developing economies. Evidence ranges from reductions to neutrality or even increases in work effort, underscoring the need to consider household conditions that shape responses. One such condition is indebtedness, a widespread feature of low-income settings that influences both consumption and labor decisions. This study examines how debt moderates the labor supply effects of Ghana's LEAP 1000 program, where beneficiary households carried higher baseline debt burdens than the rural population nationally. Using household panel data and a triple-difference design, we analyze heterogeneity by debt status, amount, type, source, and gender. Findings highlight the central role of indebtedness in conditioning program impacts. Indebted beneficiaries increase wage work, total hours, and non-farm enterprise (NFE) formation, unlike debt-free households, where income effects dominate. Gender differences are evident: male-headed households expand both wage work and self-employment, while female-headed households benefit more from formal credit, which facilitates entrepreneurship. Debt type and source also matter. Investment and consumption debt reinforce participation in wage labor and enterprise, while informal borrowing pushes households into wage work under repayment pressure. Formal credit, by contrast, enables longer-term entrepreneurial activity, particularly among women.

Does Universal Child Care Improve Health across Generations? Evidence from the Lanham Act

[Draft coming soon]

Universal child care is expanding in the United States and abroad, yet evidence on its long-run health effects comes almost entirely from small, targeted programs. I study the Lanham Act, the only universal child care program in United States history, which operated during World War II. Linking three generations in the Panel Study of Income Dynamics and exploiting birth-cohort eligibility and cross-state variation in Lanham spending, I estimate the program's long-run health effects using a difference-in-differences design. The findings indicate that exposed children reach their 50s with fewer chronic conditions, less cardiovascular disease, and modestly better self-rated health. The gains concentrate among those exposed at nursery age and are larger for men, Black adults, and children from poor families. These benefits extend to later generations: the children of exposed adults have less cardiovascular disease, and their grandchildren show suggestive gains in self-rated health. Universal care in the first years of life yields health returns that outlast the generation it serves.

Welfare Reform and Intergenerational Mobility

[Draft coming soon]

The 1996 welfare reform replaced cash entitlements with work requirements and time limits, promising that children would grow up in working families with better prospects. Evaluations of the reform have focused on mothers and on children's short-run outcomes, and studies of policy and intergenerational mobility have examined expansions of support rather than cuts. Whether the reform changed where children landed relative to their parents remains unknown. Linking mothers and children in the Panel Study of Income Dynamics and exploiting the staggered timing of state waivers and TANF across birth cohorts, I estimate how exposure at different ages shaped children's adult income ranks. Exposure before age five raised the probability that children of low-educated single mothers surpassed their mothers' income rank, while later exposure had no comparable effect. The reform raised the level of mobility without changing its persistence, as neither the rank-rank slope nor the intergenerational elasticity moved. The gains operate through marriage and spouses' earnings rather than schooling, and they do not extend to adult poverty or public assistance.

Universal Access, Divided Opinions: A Latent Class Analysis of Public Perceptions of Ghana's Free Senior High School Policy

Onyina, K. (2026). Social Policy and Society, 1–17.

Journal Google Scholar
While the abolition of secondary school fees has expanded educational access across low-income countries, empirical research has focused predominantly on student outcomes and institutional or elite perspectives, overlooking how ordinary citizens perceive such reforms or the factors shaping those perceptions. This gap is especially salient in Ghana, where the Free SHS policy, arguably the most comprehensive in Sub-Saharan Africa, is both nationally lauded and politically contested. Drawing on policy feedback theory and deservingness heuristics, this article explores how Ghanaians interpret the policy's goals, trade-offs, and implementation using a nationally representative sample of 1915 respondents. Latent Class Analysis reveals five classes, from Universal Access Champions down to Ambivalent Observers. A follow-up multinomial logistic regression shows that partisanship, education, civic engagement, and economic outlook significantly predict class membership. These findings highlight that sustaining support for large-scale reforms requires not just expanded access, but also public trust in their fairness and responsiveness.

Quantifying the Effect of Partisanship on Citizens' Collective Action Against Corruption in African Democracies: A Quasi-Experimental Approach

Oboro-Offerie, R. A., & Onyina, K. (2026). International Criminology, 6(1), 69–88.

Journal Google Scholar
In many African democracies, political parties mediate access to information, coordination, privileges, and protection, shaping citizens' beliefs about their capacity to fight corruption. While prior research has identified key drivers of collective action, less is known about how variation in partisan attachment influences such engagement. This paper asks whether non-partisanship lowers perceived collective efficacy and, if so, for whom. Drawing on Afrobarometer data Rounds 6–7 (2014–2018) from 34 democracies (N = 64,192), we estimate the relationship between party attachment and perceived collective efficacy using nearest-neighbor matching, weighted probit models, and Lewbel IV. At the aggregate level, non-partisans are less likely to believe in citizens' capacity to combat corruption. Yet disaggregated models reveal a Simpson's paradox: non-partisan men display a strong positive effect, while the effect for non-partisan women is significantly weaker; urban non-partisans express greater belief in collective efficacy, whereas rural non-partisans register diminished confidence. We interpret diminished efficacy among non-partisans as evidence of exclusion from party-mediated infrastructures, while highlighting non-partisan pathways that can foster anti-corruption participation, particularly among women and rural residents.

Unpacking the Multidimensional Impact of International Remittances on Women's Empowerment in Africa: A Quasi-Experimental Study

Onyina, K., Oboro-Offerie, R. A., & Akoto, O. D. A. (2025). SN Social Sciences, 5(10), 163.

Journal Google Scholar
While research on women's empowerment in Africa has traditionally focused on education, microfinance, and the role of feminist movements and NGOs, the gendered impacts of international remittances remain underexplored. We provide the first micro-level, cross-country evidence on how international remittances influence women's empowerment across 34 democratic African countries, drawing on Afrobarometer Round 7 data. We construct a multidimensional empowerment index — encompassing gender equality, economic agency, political participation, and health autonomy — using Multiple Correspondence Analysis, and employ entropy balancing with fractional logit models (supplemented by IPWRA for robustness). Remittances significantly enhance women's empowerment, with the strongest effects in the economic, political, and gender-equality domains. These effects are more pronounced among rural women and younger cohorts, while older women see relatively greater gains in health-related autonomy. We frame remittances as a context-dependent yet transformative channel for advancing gender equality, with implications for policies that reduce transfer costs and expand inclusive financial and social infrastructure.

Agricultural Extension Services and Household Welfare: Evidence from the Ghana Socioeconomic Panel Survey

Aremu, T., Baye, R. S., Onyina, K., & Abijah, P. (2025). Agricultural and Food Economics, 13(1), 58.

Journal Google Scholar
While agriculture is integral to the development plans of many developing countries, the sector faces several challenges, and although agricultural extension and advisory service (AEAS) is prescribed as essential, the evidence base is thin — especially in Ghana, where most studies rely on cross-sectional, regional, small-sample analyses. Using three waves of the nationally representative Ghana Socioeconomic Panel Survey, we analyze the effects of AEAS on poverty, assets, per-capita consumption, and dietary diversity. We find that AEAS is associated with a 28.3% increase in household and farm assets, a 20% increase in per-capita food consumption value, and a 4.2% increase in dietary diversity, with the largest gains among households advised by farmer-based organizations. Despite these positive effects, uptake is generally low and results for poverty are null or negative. We recommend that government strengthen the extension system and encourage uptake from both government and non-government providers.

To Report or Not to Report Corruption? An Empirical Investigation of the Determinants of Corruption Reporting in Africa

Oboro-Offerie, R. A., Allor, P. W., Toklo, S., & Onyina, K. (2025). Crime, Law and Social Change, 83(1), 1–29.

Journal Google Scholar
Corruption remains a persistent challenge in Africa, yet the individual-level factors that influence citizens' willingness to report it remain understudied, despite various national anti-corruption efforts. Using a generalised ordered logit model, we find that higher education, employment with assets, civic engagement, and trust in political institutions increase the likelihood of reporting corruption, while political affiliation, urban residency, gender, religiosity, perceptions of official impunity, and media consumption have weaker effects. These results highlight the complexity of citizens' anti-corruption efforts and the need for multifaceted strategies tailored to individual and contextual factors in Africa.

Impact of Social Protection Policies on Inclusive Growth in Sub-Saharan Africa: Evidence from a Bias-Corrected Dynamic Panel

Onyina, K., & Baye, R. S. (2024). Cogent Economics & Finance, 12(1), 2409421.

Journal Google Scholar
This study examines the role of social protection policies as a driver of inclusive growth. Using a panel of 48 sub-Saharan African countries over 1990–2022 and the least-squares dummy variable corrected (LSDVC) estimator, we find that social protection policies significantly contribute to inclusive growth, consistent with the Rawlsian theory of justice. Real effective exchange rate, foreign direct investment, CO2 emissions, and infrastructure positively influence inclusive growth, whereas corruption and inflation exert a negative effect. The study underscores the urgency of reinvigorating social protection systems to facilitate inclusive growth in the region.
